WHEN THE OTHER EAR ANSWERS

Cross-hearing simulator

A test signal can reach the opposite cochlea through the skull. Interaural attenuation (IA) is the loss along that route. Masking the non-test ear helps make responses ear-specific.

The numbers below describe a hypothetical patient. The sound is a digital illustration of routing and attenuation, not actual bone conduction or a simulation of your hearing thresholds. dB HL values never control headphone output directly.

Fixed comfortable test-route tone; crossed amplitude = test amplitude × 10−IA/20. The isolated crossed route is not boosted. It may be inaudible at high IA. Illustrative noise is not a clinical masking prescription.

Try this: predict, then listen

  1. Set presentation to 70 dB HL, IA to 40 dB, and the non-test threshold to 10 dB HL. Predict whether cross-hearing is possible, then check the readout.
  2. Change IA to 60 dB, then 70 dB. When does the estimate fall below the non-test threshold?
  3. For an audible routing illustration, lower IA to 0 dB and compare the two isolated routes. Increase IA to 10, then 20 dB and listen to the crossed route. These are demonstration values, not assumed headphone IA values.
  4. Switch test ears, then add noise to the non-test ear and compare both routes.

Keep your volume low. A crossed signal at high IA may be inaudible; do not turn it up. Hypothetical presentation and threshold settings change the calculation, not playback level.

02

SIGNAL MEETS COMPETITION

Energetic masking

Noise can obscure a tone when their excitation patterns overlap in the auditory system. Keep the tone fixed and increase the noise by reducing SNR.

SNR

10 log₁₀(signal power / noise power)

Here SNR compares tone RMS with total broadband-noise RMS over the shared listening interval. It is not noise spectrum level or a predicted detection threshold.

Predict the lowest SNR at which you can still detect the tone. Keep your device and shared playback level unchanged.

Try this: find where the tone gets lost

  1. Choose 1,000 Hz and listen to Tone alone.
  2. Set SNR to +12 dB and play Tone + noise.
  3. Try +6, 0, −6, −12, then −18 dB. Wait for playback to finish each time. At which setting does the tone become uncertain?
  4. Return to the same SNR and compare 500 Hz with 2,000 Hz. Describe what changes.

Change one setting at a time and keep volume fixed. The tone stays at the same digital RMS; lower SNR raises the noise.

03

THE ORDER MATTERS

Masking in time

Forward: noise ends before the tone. Backward: noise begins after the tone. Simultaneous (also called concurrent): noise overlaps the tone. Timing is scheduled on the audio clock.

Noise lasts 300 ms; the 1 kHz tone lasts 20 ms. Both include 5 ms onset/offset ramps. A zero gap means adjacent sounds, without overlap. In simultaneous mode the tone is centered within the noise, and gap is ignored.

Audibility varies. Forward masking is often easier to demonstrate than backward masking; failure to hear a difference is a valid observation. Non-simultaneous masking is not simply simultaneous energetic overlap.

Try this: change the gap

  1. Listen to the Reference tone to learn the short sound you are listening for.
  2. Select Forward with a 20 ms gap and play the timing example. Then compare 0, 50, and 200 ms.
  3. Select Backward and repeat the same gaps. Check the timing readout: now the tone comes first.
  4. Select Simultaneous and compare with the reference. The gap control is disabled because the sounds overlap.

Which arrangement makes the tone hardest to hear? No difference is also a valid observation, especially for backward masking. Keep volume steady.

04

MAKE A LISTENING DECISION

Find the hidden tone

Two intervals contain broadband noise. One also contains a 1 kHz tone. Choose the interval with the tone. Repeat at different SNRs and record what changes.

Listen for two 600 ms intervals, separated by 500 ms. Chance performance is 50%. This is a fixed-SNR exercise, not an adaptive clinical threshold test.

Try this: choose an interval

  1. Start at 0 dB SNR. Press Play next pair and listen for two intervals.
  2. Choose Interval 1 or Interval 2 after both sounds finish. Read the feedback.
  3. Try five pairs at the same SNR, then five pairs at −12 dB. Try −18 dB if the tone is still clear.
  4. Compare how certain you feel in the easier and harder conditions. A correct guess does not always mean you heard the tone.

Chance is 50%. The displayed total pools trials; use the per-trial feedback to discuss each condition separately. This tone task is detection, while the sentence activity involves recognition.

05

FROM AUDIBILITY TO UNDERSTANDING

Speech in noise

Explore how the same sentence sounds at −9, −6, −3, 0, +3, and +6 dB SNR. Just listen—there are no typed responses or speech scores.

What changes? Lower SNR means more babble relative to speech. Babble can produce both energetic masking and interference from competing voices (informational masking). Changing SNR alone does not isolate informational masking.

Reproducible sentence order

The same seed gives the same order of five sentences and the same babble offset for each recording. Changing the seed restarts the set.

Play selected SNR repeats the current sentence so you can compare conditions directly. Next sentence advances without repeating a recording until you restart the five-sentence set. Repetition can make a sentence easier to understand through familiarity; this is exploration, not a scored test. Keep device and shared playback levels steady.

Choose an SNR and press Play selected SNR. No response is required.

Try this: work down the SNR ladder

  1. Start at +6 dB SNR and listen to one sentence.
  2. Keep the same sentence. Listen at +3, 0, −3, −6, then −9 dB SNR, waiting for each playback to finish.
  3. Notice which speech details become harder to follow and how much effort you need. Do not increase the volume.
  4. Select Next sentence. Try the ladder in reverse, from −9 to +6 dB SNR. Does the change in clarity feel similar?

Discuss: How could knowing a sentence already change what you hear at a harder SNR?

Discuss what changed.

Which task measured detection, and which measured recognition? Did your prediction match your listening experience? How could attention, sentence difficulty, repetition, and listening order affect your impressions of clarity?
